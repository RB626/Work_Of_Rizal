About

José Protasio Rizal Mercado y Alonso Realonda was a Filipino nationalist, writer, and polymath during the tail end of the Spanish colonial period of the Philippines. He is considered the national hero of the Philippines.

An ophthalmologist by profession, Rizal became a writer and a key member of the Filipino Propaganda Movement, which advocated political reforms for the colony under Spain. He was executed by the Spanish colonial government for the crime of rebellion after the Philippine Revolution, inspired in part by his writings, broke out.

“He who does not know how to look back at where he came from will never get to his destination.”

Though he never called for violent revolution, his death made him a martyr to the cause of Philippine independence. Today, Rizal is remembered for his literary works, his advocacy for peaceful reform, and his ultimate sacrifice for his beloved homeland.

A Life of Courage and Brilliance

01
EARLY LIFE · 1861–1877

Born in Calamba

José Protacio Rizal Mercado y Alonso Realonda was born on June 19, 1861, in Calamba, Laguna, the seventh of eleven children. His father, Francisco Rizal Mercado, was a prosperous farmer; his mother, Teodora Alonso, was an educated woman who nurtured his love of learning. By age 8, he had already written his first poem in Tagalog.

02
EDUCATION · 1877–1882

Scholar in Manila

Rizal entered the Ateneo Municipal de Manila at age 11, earning a Bachelor of Arts with the highest honors. He then studied at the University of Santo Tomas, pursuing medicine with the goal of curing his mother's worsening eyesight. Finding the friars' teaching backward, he resolved to continue his studies in Europe.

03
EUROPE & WORKS · 1882–1892

Reformist in Exile

In Madrid, Rizal finished his degree in medicine and philosophy. He traveled through Spain, France, Germany, Austria, and Switzerland — learning ophthalmology under Dr. Otto Becker in Heidelberg. During this decade, he wrote his landmark novels Noli Me Tángere (1887) and El Filibusterismo (1891), which electrified the Filipino reform movement.

04
EXILE · 1892–1896

Dapitan and Bagumbayan

Returning to the Philippines in 1892, Rizal founded La Liga Filipina — a civic organization for peaceful reform — and was swiftly arrested and exiled to Dapitan, Mindanao. There he practiced medicine, taught, built infrastructure, and continued writing. After four years, he was arrested on charges of rebellion and executed by firing squad on December 30, 1896, at Bagumbayan (now Rizal Park), aged 35.

The Rizal Family

José Rizal came from a prosperous and educated family in Calamba, Laguna. His parents instilled in him a love for learning, culture, and the Filipino people. He was the seventh of eleven children in a family that valued education and intellectual pursuits.

Francisco Mercado Rizal

FATHER (1818-1898)

A prosperous farmer and the town's wealthiest man. Francisco was a graduate of the Colegio de San José in Manila. He was known for his business acumen and was able to provide his children with excellent education. He supported José's studies in Europe despite the risks involved.

Teodora Alonso Realonda

MOTHER (1827-1911)

An educated and cultured woman who was José's first teacher. She taught him to read and write, and nurtured his love for poetry and the arts. Despite being imprisoned for two years on false charges, she remained a pillar of strength for the family. José's deep devotion to her influenced his decision to study ophthalmology to cure her failing eyesight.

Josefa Rizal

"Panggoy" • 1865-1945

An epileptic who remained unmarried. She was very close to José and helped in his reform activities.

Soledad Rizal

"Choleng" • 1870-1929

The youngest child who married Pantaleon Quintero. She was also very devoted to José.

Paciano Rizal

"Paciano" • 1851-1930

José's older brother and closest confidant. He financed José's education and was a revolutionary general. He sacrificed his own education to support José.

Olympia Rizal

"Ypia" • 1855-1887

Married Silvestre Ubaldo. She died in 1887, the same year Noli Me Tángere was published.

Maria Rizal

"Biang" • 1859-1945

Married Daniel Faustino Cruz. She was known for her business acumen and managed the family affairs.

Concepcion Rizal

"Concha" • 1862-1865

Died at age three. Her death deeply affected young José and was his first encounter with grief.

Trinidad Rizal

"Trining" • 1868-1951

The one to whom José entrusted his final poem hidden in an alcohol lamp on the eve of his execution.

Family Values & Influence

Education

The Rizal family highly valued education. Most of the siblings were well-educated, and they ensured that all children, including daughters, received proper schooling.

Patriotism

The family's love for the Philippines was evident in their support for José's reform efforts and Paciano's participation in the revolution.

Faith & Resilience

Despite persecution and hardships, including Teodora's imprisonment and José's execution, the family remained united and strong in their convictions.

Literary Works

Noli Me Tángere
1887 · Berlin, Germany

His first novel brutally exposed the abuses of Spanish colonial rule and the Catholic friars. The title, Latin for "Touch Me Not," follows the idealistic Crisostomo Ibarra confronting systemic injustice.

El Filibusterismo
1891 · Ghent, Belgium

The darker sequel explores themes of revolution and vengeance. Ibarra returns as Simoun, plotting violent revolution. Questions whether colonial injustice can be corrected through peaceful means.

Mi Último Adiós
1896 · Fort Santiago, Manila

His final poem, written the night before execution and hidden in an oil lamp. "My Last Farewell" is considered one of the greatest poems in Philippine literature.

Essays & Articles
1880-1891 · Various

Wrote prolifically for La Solidaridad newspaper. Key essays include "Sobre la Indolencia de los Filipinos" and "Filipinas Dentro de Cien Años" which prophesied independence.
